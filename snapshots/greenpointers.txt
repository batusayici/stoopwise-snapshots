title.rendered: Greenpoint Writer Gabrielle Moss on the ‘Midlife Plot Twist’ and Letting Go of Life’s Checklist
link: https://greenpointers.com/2026/09/03/greenpoint-writer-gabrielle-moss-on-the-midlife-plot-twist-and-letting-go-of-lifes-checklist/
date: 2026-09-03T11:30:00
content.rendered: No matter where you live, it can feel like there is a universal checklist for adulthood. You should land the killer job, find the beautiful apartment, get married, and someday own a place of your own. In Greenpoint, where many residents arrived young, single, and determined to make those expectations a reality, adulthood doesn’t always unfold according to society’s plan. Gabrielle Moss and co-author Lindsay Goldwert explore that moment of reckoning in their new book, The Midlife Plot Twist , a gentler framing of what is usually referred to as a midlife crisis. Forget the familiar image of a man buying a red Corvette. Moss and Goldwert examine how women’s crises can look less like a flashy escape and more like an internal realization that the life they built no longer reflects what they want or value. Drawing on an anonymous survey of 150 women and roughly 30 in-depth interviews, The Midlife Plot Twist makes the case that feeling lost, burnt out, or dissatisfied in midlife is not necessarily a sign that someone has failed. It can be the beginning of a long-overdue reassessment. In a conversation with Greenpointers , Moss discussed the pressure on women to appear endlessly capable, the myth that life should eventually “settle,” and the pandemic walks through Transmitter Park that gave her the space to think about all of it. You’ve been in Greenpoint for a long time. How has the neighborhood fit into the different stages of your life? I’ve been in Greenpoint for 17 years, since my late twenties. I’ve gone from being a single young woman mindlessly following dating advice in my twenties to writing this book, all from the same apartment in Greenpoint. There are a lot of little patches of grass, these almost secret places where you can hide by yourself and think in Greenpoint. I found those during the pandemic, and I kept going back afterward. Was there anything in the survey that made you think, ‘Wow, I didn’t expect people to say that’? I was surprised by how many women said they thought about burning their lives down. More than 70 percent of the women in the survey said they had thought about just getting in the car and going. I think I had absorbed the propaganda that every other woman was doing great. You look at Instagram and see the perfect family, the perfect life, and assume everyone else is happy and settled. But being confused, feeling like you made the wrong choices, or not knowing what comes next is actually very common. If you could say one thing to someone who feels, ‘I did my life wrong,’ what would it be? Your life is not over, so you didn’t do it wrong. You’re still here. We grow up with this messaging that once you reach a certain age, your life is set in stone—like you’re supposed to have figured everything out by 29. That just isn’t true. Change in your forties or fifties may be different than change in your twenties, but it isn’t necessarily worse. You probably have more responsibilities, but you also have much more information about who you are. You have data now. A lot of people reach midlife living with the consequences of decisions they made when they didn’t have enough information about what they value, what makes them happy, or what kind of life suits them. That doesn’t mean they failed. It means they made the best guesses they could at the time. Do you think men should read the book too? I hope a lot of this would work for anyone, regardless of gender. A lot of it is about asking: What are your values? Who are you? It shouldn’t feel scary to find out who you are. It can be great. But we focused on women because there are social realities that make this experience different. Women are still, by and large, doing more childcare and caregiving. In our research, we found that the average person caring for an elderly parent in this country is a 51-year-old woman. Women are also socialized to please everyone in a way that men often aren’t. There can be enormous pressure to be a caretaker and a giver, and at some point women may hit a breaking point where they realize they have given so much that there is nothing left to give. The book is partly about creating a place to begin considering yourself again. Who do you look to now as a role model, or simply as someone whose approach to life you admire? I’ve become very interested in oddball older women, people who decided much earlier in life that the standards for how you’re supposed to live are meaningless, and who genuinely went their own way. I admire people whose success isn’t about becoming a business mastermind or making passive income online, but about living an entire life on their own terms and being honest in their work and in themselves. Greenpointers: Tell us about yourself and how you got your start in the music industry. Brian Stearns: I grew up in California, in a musical family. My mother was a singer in gospel groups in church — she sang to me since before I can remember, and people tell me I inherited her voice. I had a great-uncle named Jimmy Thomasson who was a country and western musician. In the 1940s, he played mandolin and fiddle for Louisiana governor and singer Jimmie Davis, who is famous for writing the song “You Are My Sunshine.” My whole childhood, I was around my uncle playing and singing. On holidays and special occasions, it was normal for the whole family to join in, and by the time I was 17, I was playing and joining in too. I started playing in bands as a teenager, and in my twenties, I joined a band called The Autumns, whom I’d met in high school and often played on the same bill with. They already had an album out, and I went on several US tours with them. We spent a summer in London and cut a second album at the Cocteau Twins’ studio, September Sound — but before the album was released, the label went bankrupt. It was very disappointing, but also a common experience for aspiring bands. A bit like Spinal Tap — part comedy, part tragedy. After The Autumns, I applied to California Institute of the Arts. There, I was immersed in Indian, West African, and Balinese/Javanese gamelan music, among many other musical traditions. In my children’s music recordings, I particularly like to use percussion instruments from different parts of the world — tablas, djembes, darbukas — and that all started at CalArts. I was exposed to West African music directly from teachers Kobla Ladzekpo and Alfred Ladzekpo, CalArts musicians and dancers from Ghana. From them, I learned songs sung in the Akan/Twi language group — one of my recent class additions, “Che Che Koolay,” is one of them. When I moved to NYC, I got a job at a Montessori preschool called the Children’s Garden Studio in Manhattan, under a director named Kia Woods. In the world of children’s education and music, Kia was my Yoda. She wrote the song “Hurrah Hooray, It’s Music Time Today,” which was a theme song for the school, and I’ve carried it on as Tunes for Tykes’ theme song ever since. In my classes, I still follow the format I learned from the Children’s Garden — welcome circle, sign language, instrument sharing and cleanup, exploring seasonal themes and lessons, guided movement, storytime, goodbye circle. To everything there is a season, and a time for every purpose. Brian Stearns at one of his classes in McCarren Park. Photo: Tunes for Tykes Greenpointers: How did Tunes for Tykes get its start? Brian Stearns: The very first classes I gave independently were in Crown Heights, at an ice cream shop called Candy Rush. But it started in earnest at Rough Trade Records in 2015. Store manager George Flanagan came up with the name “Tunes for Tykes” and invited me to give classes there one day a week. Over the first several years, classes expanded slowly but steadily. By 2019, I was teaching five days a week at Rough Trade (now closed) , the Flying Squirrel (now closed) , and Edamama (568 Union Ave.). Then, in 2020, COVID hit. For the first few months, I offered Zoom classes online, but soon families were reaching out, urging me to come play outside. At the time, I lived in Williamsburg, so I decided to give it a try. It was early July 2020, and I had 50 kids show up on that first day — overwhelming but beautiful, and that’s how the McCarren Music Circle was born. Six years later, it’s still going strong. It’s been very sweet watching families grow — some starting with one baby and returning when siblings come along. It built a sense of community and bonding during a time when that was much needed. Now, I teach at four indoor locations across Greenpoint and Williamsburg, as well as two days a week in McCarren Park. Brian Stearns at one of his classes. Photo: Tunes for Tykes Greenpointers: What do you love most about singing to kids? Brian Stearns: Their joy is contagious — it’s a beautiful gift of giving and receiving. When they get excited, I get excited, and over time, it’s helped me learn what they respond to. I love seeing how they recognize and learn the songs and dances over their time with me, and how they pick up on the musical cues before they can even speak — the overall rhythm and process of the class. I work on personal music from time to time. As an artist, my favorite is when that world collides with my work in children’s music, like with “Sound of the Bells,” which is a very personal song, but also written for the children and the experience of playing for them.
